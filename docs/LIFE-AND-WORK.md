# Life and work

I am Paul Fleury, a French internet entrepreneur based in Lisbon ([professional profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/paulfxyz/)). This is a deliberately selective account of my work and outlook, not an exhaustive autobiography.

## A life in different places

Over the past 15 years, I have lived in France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Germany, Mexico, the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, Thailand, Spain, Italy, and Portugal. That list is in no particular chronological order, and it may not be exhaustive: there could be one or two more places to add.

Each country on that list represents at least six months of living there, not simply a holiday or a brief visit. I am not publishing a country-by-country timeline, exact dates, or more specific lengths of stay.

I genuinely enjoyed living in every one of those places. Each city, region, and country was special and enriching in its own way, and I would not want a short biography to flatten those experiences into a list of destinations.

Living somewhere gives it a different place in your life than visiting it. There is time for ordinary routines, for unfamiliar things to become familiar, and for a place to mean more than its most recognisable sights. That everyday dimension is an important part of what I value about these experiences.

Nor do I think each country needs to stand for a neat lesson or a national stereotype. What stays with me is the variety: different surroundings, different ways of doing things, and the pleasure of discovering that more than one place can feel meaningful. I am grateful for the time I spent in all of them.

Lisbon is my current base ([professional profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/paulfxyz/)). It gives the story a present-day starting point, alongside the many other places that have been part of my life.

## The work today

Openline is my main business, and my role brings together product direction, operations, and the work of specialist teams around an eSIM connectivity service ([professional profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/paulfxyz/), [Openline](https://openline.com)). Around it, Megastack, Calliope, and Asymptote reflect my interests in security, practical AI, company building, and advisory work ([professional profile](https://www.linkedin.com/in/paulfxyz/)).

There is also a different kind of work: tools and experiments that begin with a problem I actually want to solve ([personal portfolio](https://paulfleury.com)). A place to put a thought, a way to monitor a service, a command-line helper, or a better news briefing can be a worthwhile project without becoming an all-consuming company.

Different projects ask for different kinds of commitment. Running a business, working with a client, exploring a public-good idea, and releasing a small tool carry different responsibilities. The [project index](PROJECTS.md) makes those distinctions easier to understand.

What interests me is the connection between an idea and something that is actually useful. That includes deciding what to keep simple, what to leave out, and when to bring in somebody with deeper expertise.

## Building with AI, taking responsibility as a person

The Weekathon presents AI-assisted products as things to try, inspect, improve, and learn from ([Weekathon](https://ph.paulfleury.com)). It is a launch experiment, not a claim that a single person independently hand-wrote every component, or that seven products were built from nothing in seven days ([Weekathon](https://ph.paulfleury.com)).

I use “Chief Hacking Officer” as a way to describe an organisational capability: finding useful problems, turning them into owned tools, and staying responsible for the result ([Weekathon](https://ph.paulfleury.com)). The point is not to replace every subscription with an unexamined AI bill, but to make deliberate choices about ordinary software, appropriate models, operating costs, security, and maintenance ([Weekathon](https://ph.paulfleury.com)).

Code is only part of that work. I care about whether the interface makes sense, whether people can use it in their language, and whether somebody can maintain it after the launch-day excitement has passed.

I also want the claims around a project to stay proportionate to what exists. A working experiment can be interesting without being called a revolution; a launch can be worth sharing without pretending that testing, support, and improvement are finished.

## A public-good direction

Enki is a separate public-good initiative concerned with decentralised, local-first AI and more practical control over the tools people depend on ([Enki](https://enki.ngo)). Its proposals and ambitions should not be confused with a claim that every part of its roadmap already exists.

That distinction matters to me more broadly. An experiment can have a clear direction without pretending that the work is finished.

## What connects the different chapters

The country list, the career history, and the projects are different chapters of one life. I like leaving room for curiosity, changes of direction, and the value of an experience in its own right.

Living in different places has been personally enriching; building things gives me a way to turn curiosity into work. I prefer a practical account of myself: interested, involved, still learning, and responsible for what I choose to put into the world.

## A life larger than its public record

I prefer to be known through useful work rather than continuous personal exposure. I am not making a lifetime promise to avoid a more open presence; I understand that a changing world increasingly expects some visibility as part of social trust.

For now, I keep that exposure bounded. The [personal statement](PERSONAL-STATEMENT.md) explains the health and privacy context I have chosen to share, and the [portrait guide](PORTRAIT.md) sets out the single image I permit others to reuse.

The point of this kit is to make an introduction easier, not to fill every gap in the public record. There is a full personal life behind the work, and some of it can remain personal without diminishing how much I enjoyed it or how much it has meant to me.
